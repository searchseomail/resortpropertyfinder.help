Help: Discrepancies between cadastral map and physical boundaries in Bulgaria

faded_glitch
Topic Starter
Posted: 24.01.2025 10:35
I am currently in the process of purchasing a rural property in Bulgaria, but my surveyor discovered a significant mismatch between the official cadastral map and the actual fenced boundaries on the ground. The seller claims the fence has been in the same place for 30 years, but I am worried about potential legal claims from neighboring landowners. Has anyone dealt with a similar discrepancy, and what is the best way to rectify these boundary issues before finalizing the notary deed? I want to ensure my investment is secure and avoid future litigation with neighbors.

Replies (12)

arctic_fox_99

This is a classic 'cadastral error' issue in Bulgaria. Do not sign the notary deed until you have a 'geodetic expert' provide a formal opinion. If the fence has been there for 30 years, it might be a case of 'acquisitive prescription', but you need a lawyer to verify the neighbor's position first.
blaze_horizon
Replied: 15.04.2025 08:28
I dealt with this last year. My advice: make the seller responsible for fixing the cadastral map before the final payment. If you buy it as-is, the problem becomes yours, and the Geodesy, Cartography and Cadastre Agency can take months to process corrections.

Before you get too deep into the legalities of the land, make sure you are fully aware of the regulations if you aren't a Bulgarian citizen. Check out this guide on how to buy property in Bulgaria as a foreigner to ensure all your paperwork is in order.
quiet_storm
Replied: 02.06.2025 09:30
The '30-year fence' argument is common, but it doesn't automatically grant legal ownership if the cadastral map says otherwise. You need a formal boundary protocol signed by all neighbors. If they refuse to sign, walk away. It is not worth the risk of future litigation.
lunar_orbit
Replied: 03.06.2025 08:40
Definitely hire a local lawyer who specializes in property law. They can perform a 'due diligence' search on the property's history. Don't rely on the seller's surveyor—get your own independent expert to verify the coordinates.
ember_glow
Replied: 05.06.2025 11:53
If the fence is encroaching on a neighbor's plot, the seller is currently in a state of 'unjust enrichment'. Do not finalize the purchase until the boundary is officially adjusted in the cadastre, otherwise, you might be forced to move the fence later at your own expense.

It is quite common in rural Bulgaria. If the discrepancy is small, sometimes neighbors sign a mutual agreement, but for a significant mismatch, you need an administrative procedure to correct the 'technical error' in the cadastral plan.

Ask the seller for the 'sketch' (skitsa) of the property. If the sketch doesn't match the reality, the notary should technically refuse to sign the deed until it is corrected. If they are pushing you to close, they are hiding a potential conflict.
kinetic_pulse
Replied: 21.08.2025 06:09
Don't trust verbal promises from the seller about the neighbors being 'fine with it'. Once you buy the land, the neighbors might suddenly have a change of heart or sell their property to someone who will demand you move the fence. Get everything in writing.

You should request a 'geodetic survey' that includes the neighboring plots. This will show exactly how much land is being 'stolen' or 'lost'. If the gap is significant, use it as leverage to lower the purchase price or demand the seller covers the cost of the cadastre correction.
